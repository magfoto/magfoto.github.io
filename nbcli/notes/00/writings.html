Section 2.1 -> 2.11

Transformation is generally defined as "a thorough or dramatic change in form and appearance" where, in electronic music composition can refer to sound transformation (Roads 2015, Chapter V., 110), or in biology, refer to the shape and anatomy of plants and animals (Thompson 1917). Biologist and botanist D'Arcy Thompson wrote the canonical text On Growth and Form (Ibid.), a body of studies on organic form, considered a foundation on general morphology. In this text, Thompson creates a theory of transformation (Ibid, Chapter XVII., 719), demonstrating that organic forms, beyond heredity, are shaped by the movement of matter, and therefore formed by physical forces.

This line of thinking continues in architecture, as On Growth and Form influenced Greg Lynn's work on "architectural curvilinearity" creating a new form of computational architecture popularly referred to as animate architecture (Kolarevic 2000). Animate architecture is a design concept driven by motion kinematics and dynamics, work which Lynn further developed in his book Animate Form (Lynn 1999).

A kinematic diagram, used in mechanical engineering for drawing the connections between parts of a mechanism, can be created as a graph, like the kinematic diagrams of Xenakis (Xenakis 1992, 72), for which he also refers to as a transition diagram.

For Xenakis, transformations themselves are mechanisms, whether biological or physical, under these five conditions:

  1. Each state of the mechanism is in one-to-one correspondence with a term of the transformation.
  2. Each sequence of states crossed by the mechanism by reason of its internal structure corresponds to an uninterrupted sequence of the terms of the transformation.
  3. If the mechanism reaches a state and remains there (absorbing or stationary state), the term which corresponds to this state has no transform.
  4. If the states of a mechanism reproduce themselves in the same manner without end, the transformation has kinematic diagram in closed circuit.
  5. If the states of the mechanism and its start from another state is represented in the diagram by a displacement of the representative point, which is not due to an arrow but to an arbitrary action on the paper.

Xenakis focused these conditions on the making of Markovian stochastic music, and based his theory of transformation in stochastic music on states; states of matrices and their translations. Thompson's theory of transformation, however was based on a method of coordinates and deformation. It can also be deduced that Xenakis used an approach that was statistical in nature, whereas Thompson used the Cartesian plane, a geometric approach. He took deformation to be a less complicated phenomenon of comprehension, and this was acceptable as morphology was more concerned with comparison rather than precise definition (Thompson 1917, 723).

In his book Composing Electronic Music (Roads 2015), Curtis Roads discusses sound transformation as variation, composition development, and contrasts differences such as source-cause transformation versus spectromorphological transformation, mutation versus transmutation, and the various control regimes in sound transformation. Roads uses the analogy of phase transitions of solid to liquid to gas to plasma, to introduce the perception thresholds we possess that he refers to as zones of morphosis. They are aesthetic qualities that occur when we notice a qualitative change, that we identify as a listener (Roads 2015, 120). He presents the popular example of the simple crossfade as one of those zones, that gives a mutation effect to the listener, and based simply on the interplay of amplitudes of two sound signals. This form of analysis of transformation is of interest for its potential for testing new instruments's capability to facilitate these zones of morphosis.
